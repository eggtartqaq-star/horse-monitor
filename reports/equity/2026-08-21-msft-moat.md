## 2. The trap: why "wide moat" is not an answer

The brief is correct and I will not waste the Owner's time confirming what he already believes.
Everyone agrees Microsoft has a wide moat.

So state the question properly. **The interesting question about Microsoft in 2026 is not
whether the moat is wide. It is whether the moat is being *bought* rather than *earned*.**

A moat, in this house's definition, is a structural feature that lets a business earn returns far
above its cost of capital *without proportionate reinvestment*. That is the entire point: the
fortress is already built and its maintenance capex is small relative to the rent it collects.

What Microsoft is doing right now — **~$116bn of capex in FY2026, up ~80% year on year, and
roughly $175bn of calendar-2026 capital spending planned** — is the behaviour of a company that
must *purchase* its competitive position annually, in cash, in a capital-intensive, physically
depreciating, commodity-input-exposed asset class whose key input (memory) is itself in a price
spike.

**The software moat and the AI-infrastructure moat are not the same moat, and the second is
structurally worse than the first.** Software licences scale at near-zero marginal cost; GPU
clusters do not. §3 shows this is already visible in the returns, and it is visible *despite*
accounting changes that push in the opposite direction (§3.6).

The rest of this report answers one question: **is a high-return enterprise software franchise
subsidising a low-return AI infrastructure land-grab, and if so, is the Owner being asked to pay
a software multiple for a business that is becoming a hybrid?**

## 3. The ROIC gate — no moat without it

My standing rule: no moat without ROIC evidence; popularity is not a moat. Microsoft passes on
level with room to spare. **The trajectory is the single most important finding in this report.**

The three vendors disagree on the FY2026 point estimate by nearly six percentage points. I do
not have a primary invested-capital reconciliation and I will not pretend to one (§15, item 1).
**But all three agree on the direction, and the direction has been down in every single year
since FY2021.** A six-point disagreement on the level does not touch a twenty-point move in the
trend.

Now the cost of capital. GuruFocus computes **WACC of 12.06% as of June 2026, against a 10-year
median of 9.51% and a 10-year range of 5.61%–12.06% — i.e. Microsoft's cost of capital is at its

**The spread is compressing from both ends at once.**

| | FY2021 | FY2026 |
|---|---|---|
| ROIC | ~45.6% | ~20.5–26.1% |
| WACC | nearer the ~9.5% decade median *(my inference from the stated median and range — an estimate, not a sourced point)* | **12.06% — decade high** |
| **ROIC − WACC spread** | **~36pp** | **~8–14pp** |

**A spread of 8–14 points is still a moat.** It is not nothing — most businesses never earn
their cost of capital at all, and 0100.HK could not demonstrate a positive spread on any
measure. But it is **a quarter to a third of the spread the same company earned five years ago,
and the compression is monotonic, not cyclical.**

**The economic commitment did not fall by $15bn. The reported capex line did.** Anyone who tells
the committee "capex is moderating" must be asked to check the lease mix first. I flag this as a
presentation risk requiring verification against the FY2026 10-K lease footnote (§15, item 2). I
am not alleging impropriety; I am saying the metric changed definition mid-race.

### 3.4 It is showing up in the numerator too — margins

Management's own attribution: compression was "driven by a sales mix shift to Azure as well as

ongoing efficiency gains, particularly in Azure and M365 Commercial cloud."

**Read that attribution carefully, because it is an admission.** My mandate names
*stable-or-rising gross margins* as moat evidence. Microsoft's are falling, and management's own
explanation is that **the fastest-growing part of the business is the structurally
lower-margin part.** That is the definition of negative mix. The growth is entirely real; it is
arriving at a worse gross margin than the franchise it is diluting.

### 3.5 Verdict on the ROIC gate

**PASSES ON LEVEL AND ON DURATION. FAILS THE "STABLE-OR-RISING GROSS MARGIN" TEST OUTRIGHT.
TRAJECTORY NEGATIVE ON BOTH ROIC AND GROSS MARGIN, MONOTONICALLY, FOR FIVE YEARS.**

Stated without hedging: **on the two quantitative tests my mandate specifies, Microsoft passes
the first and fails the second.** The moat is wide. It is also — measured the only way this desk
permits it to be measured — **narrower than it was in 2021 and getting narrower each year.**

### 3.6 The finding that makes 3.2 worse, not better

Microsoft has **extended the depreciable useful life of server and network equipment from 4 to 6
years**, and is **extending the estimated useful life of datacentres and office buildings from
15 to 25 years, effective at the start of FY27**
([ScanX](https://scanx.trade/stock-market-news/companies/microsoft-extends-data-center-useful-life-to-25-years/46916505);
[The Investing Engineer](https://investingengineer.com/microsoft-fiscal-q4-2026-earnings-report/);
[MBI Deep Dives on Big Tech earnings quality](https://www.mbi-deepdives.com/big-tech-earnings-quality/)).

**Useful-life extensions reduce depreciation, raise reported NOPAT, and therefore *flatter*
ROIC.** The point that matters for this report is directional and I want it stated plainly:

> **Microsoft's ROIC fell from 45.6% to ~20–26% *while* the accounting was being changed in a
> direction that pushes ROIC up. The underlying deterioration is therefore larger than the
> reported series shows.**

This is my highest-conviction non-consensus concern and I have not seen it framed this way
anywhere in the sourcing. A 25-year useful life on a building housing hardware that is
technologically obsolete in five is an assumption the committee should interrogate, not accept.
Whether it is aggressive is Dept 2's financial-statements analyst's call (巴爺爺), not mine —
**I am flagging it as a referral, not adjudicating it** (§15, item 3).

## 4. Moat source 1 — Enterprise switching costs and the bundle

**Rating: WIDE. Trajectory: STABLE, with the first credible erosion vector in three decades
visible at the edges. This is the core moat and it is still intact.**

### 4.1 The evidence the lock-in is real — the best evidence in the report

My mandate defines pricing power precisely: **price increases that do not cost share.** For most
companies that is hard to isolate. At Microsoft in FY2026 it is directly observable, because the
company discloses seats and revenue separately.

| Metric, FY2026 | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| M365 Commercial cloud **revenue** growth | Q1 **+17%**, Q2 **+17%**, Q3 **+19%**, Q4 **+16%** | Microsoft IR Productivity & Business Processes pages ([Q1](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/earnings/fy-2026-q1/productivity-and-business-processes-performance), [Q2](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/earnings/fy-2026-q2/productivity-and-business-processes-performance), [Q3](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/earnings/fy-2026-q3/productivity-and-business-processes-performance)) |
| M365 Commercial **seat** growth | **+6%**, driven by SMB and frontline-worker offerings | same |
| **Implied ARPU growth** | **~+10 to +13pp**, attributed by Microsoft to **E5 upgrades and M365 Copilot** | *my derivation from the two rows above — marked as a derivation* |
| M365 commercial paid seats | **>450 million** (FY26 Q2, Jan 2026) | [Microsoft Community Hub](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/discussions/microsoft-365/microsoft-365-exceeds-450-million-commercial-paid-seats/4490792); [Office365ITPros](https://office365itpros.com/2026/01/30/microsoft-fy26-q2-results/) |
| Entra ID monthly active users | **>300 million** *(Microsoft 2024 licensing documentation — dated, single-source, low confidence)* | [CyberOne](https://cyberone.security/blog/microsoft-entra-id-the-strategic-guide-to-identity-access-management-in-2026) |

**This is the single strongest moat datum in the report.** Microsoft raised revenue per user by
roughly ten points *while the installed base grew 6%*. Customers paid materially more per seat
and **bought more seats anyway.**

That is not "popularity." It is the signature of switching costs: **the customer's alternative to
paying more is a migration they have decided not to undertake.**

Compare the failure mode. A business with a weakening moat raises price and loses units; the
revenue line holds for two years and then rolls over. Microsoft raised price and *gained* units.
**On the specific test my mandate sets, this passes cleanly, and I am not going to soften it to
make the report sound more sceptical.**

### 4.2 The forward-commitment evidence — and the asterisk on it

| Metric, Q4 FY2026 | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Commercial remaining performance obligation (RPO)** | **$678bn, +84%** | [ScanX](https://scanx.trade/stock-market-news/companies/microsoft-q4-results-commercial-rpo-hits-record-678-billion/46990028); [Microsoft FY26 Q4 call](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/events/fy-2026/earnings-fy-2026-q4) |
| **RPO excluding OpenAI** | **+25%** | same |
| RPO weighted average duration (incl. OpenAI) | **2.3 years** | same |
| ~30% recognised within 12 months | **+37% YoY**; beyond-12-month portion **+112%** | same |
| Commercial bookings | **+18% ex-OpenAI; +10% including OpenAI** | same |

**Two things must be said and they point in opposite directions.**

*In Microsoft's favour:* **+25% RPO growth and +18% bookings growth excluding OpenAI**, on a base
this size, is genuine multi-year contractual lock-in from ordinary enterprises. Management stated
all *sequential* commercial RPO growth came from customers **outside** frontier-model companies.
That is the moat working, and it is a strong number.

*Against:* **the headline is +84% and the underlying is +25%.** A single counterparty accounts
for roughly three-fifths of the reported growth in Microsoft's flagship forward-visibility
metric. **Anyone quoting "$678bn RPO, +84%" to the committee without the ex-OpenAI figure is
quoting a number about OpenAI, not a number about Microsoft's moat.** Note also that bookings
grew *slower* including OpenAI (+10%) than excluding it (+18%) — the OpenAI contribution to
*bookings* was a drag this quarter even as it inflated RPO. See §7.

Note also the **2.3-year weighted average duration.** This is a *medium*-duration backlog, not a
regulated-utility contract book. Roughly 30% converts within twelve months. **The lock-in is
strong but repriced on a roughly three-year cadence — precisely the cadence on which a determined
enterprise CIO can plan an exit.**

### 4.3 Is the lock-in weakening? Real migration evidence, not vendor marketing

The brief asked for real migration cases. **They exist, they are documented, they are
government-led, and they are concentrated in Europe.**

| Case | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Schleswig-Holstein (German state)** | LibreOffice migration **~80% complete**; **>€15m licence savings in 2026** against a **one-time €9m** investment — **payback under one year** | [It's FOSS](https://itsfoss.com/news/german-state-ditch-microsoft/) |
| **Denmark** | Ministry of Digital Affairs switching from Microsoft to LibreOffice; Collabora pilot from mid-2025, full implementation targeted **2026** | [The Record](https://therecord.media/denmark-digital-agency-microsoft-digital-independence) |
| **Aggregate European public sector** | Denmark, Germany, France, Italy, Austria migrating **800,000+ government workstations** off Windows/Office | [2-data](https://2-data.com/knowledge-hub/a-search-for-digital-sovereignty-eu-governments-shift-from-microsoft-to-linux-libreoffice/); [Linux Journal](https://www.linuxjournal.com/content/libreoffice-drives-europes-open-source-shift-growing-push-digital-sovereignty) |
| **Driver** | Digital sovereignty / US CLOUD Act exposure — now "a mainstream political priority, backed by parliamentary resolutions and continental policy frameworks," not a technical preference | [Border Cyber Group](https://bordercybergroup.com/the-unravelling-how-europe-is-dismantling-three-decades-of-microsoft-dependency/) |

**Now the honest arithmetic, which bearish write-ups on this topic routinely omit. 800,000
workstations against >450 million M365 commercial paid seats is 0.18%.** As a revenue event this
is a rounding error and I will not dress it up as anything else.

**But it is the wrong way to read it, and here is my erosion call — made deliberately before the
numbers show it, per this desk's KPI:**

1. **The Schleswig-Holstein economics are published and they work.** €15m annual saving against a
   €9m one-off, payback under one year. Until now the honest case against migration was that it
   cost more than it saved. **A worked, public, sub-one-year payback destroys that argument as a
   general matter.** Every European public-sector CIO now has a reference case with numbers
   attached — and reference cases are what switching costs are actually made of.
2. **The motivation is political, not economic, which makes it price-insensitive.** Microsoft can
   discount its way out of a cost objection. It cannot discount its way out of the CLOUD Act.
   **This is the first time in thirty years that the decisive variable in an Office displacement
   decision has been one Microsoft does not control.**
3. **Public-sector migrations build the ecosystem that makes private-sector migration feasible** —
   trained integrators, document-format conformance, migration tooling, procurement templates.
   The switching cost of any platform is partly a function of how many others have already left.

**Counter-evidence, stated at equal strength.** Seats grew 6% globally in FY2026 *while all of
this was happening.* Whatever Europe is doing is being swamped by SMB and frontline-worker
additions elsewhere. And the migrations are overwhelmingly **Office document editing** — the most
substitutable layer — not Entra ID, not Exchange, not Azure, not the Windows/AD management
estate. **I found no published case study of a large organisation migrating off Microsoft
*identity*.** That is where the real lock-in sits and it is untouched.

### 4.4 Verdict on the bundle: it is a stack, and it is being attacked top-down

Ranked by how hard each layer is to remove, hardest first:

1. **Entra ID / identity and device management** — near-immovable. No documented large-scale
   displacement found anywhere in my sourcing.
2. **Exchange / Teams / the compliance and eDiscovery estate** — very hard; regulatory
   record-keeping dependencies compound the lock.
3. **Azure enterprise workloads** — hard, and made *artificially* harder by licensing terms now
   the subject of three regulatory actions (§9).
4. **Windows on the desktop** — hard but demonstrably doable (Schleswig-Holstein).
5. **Office document editing** — **the soft layer**, and the layer that both LibreOffice
   migrations *and* generative AI attack.

**The attack is coming at the substitutable layer first.** That is the correct place to watch and
it is not yet in the numbers. **Rating for this moat source: WIDE.**

share-of-incremental-growth basis — which is what determines share three years out — Azure lost
ground in the June 2026 quarter.**

*On attribution — AI:* I could not obtain a clean AI-versus-non-AI split of Azure revenue and
will not estimate one (§15, item 4). What I can establish is that Microsoft's disclosed **$37bn AI
annual run rate** (Q3 FY26, **+123% YoY**) is a company-wide aggregate that per the sourcing
**"aggregates Copilot licenses, Azure AI services, and passed-through OpenAI models"**
([StartupHub](https://www.startuphub.ai/ai-news/ai-figures/2026/figure-satya-nadella-company-financial-breakdown-2026-07-24);
[LicenseQ](https://licenseq.com/microsoft-fy26-q3-results-explained/)).

**"Passed-through OpenAI models" means a portion of the AI revenue Microsoft reports is OpenAI
spending money — some of it Microsoft's own invested capital — on Microsoft's cloud.** That is
not moat revenue; it is circular. I cannot size it and that gap is the highest-priority open item
after the ROIC reconciliation.

*On attribution — bundling:* here the evidence is unambiguous, and it is the honest answer.
Microsoft's licensing terms make Windows Server and SQL Server **four to five times more
expensive** to run on AWS or Google Cloud than on Azure
([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/09/25/google_microsoft_cloud_complaint/);
[Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366612012/Google-Cloud-files-complaint-with-European-Commission-over-Microsofts-cloud-licensing-practices)).

**A four-to-five-times price differential imposed by licence terms is not a moat. It is a toll.
Tolls are what regulators remove** (§9). To the extent Azure's share gain is attributable to that
mechanism rather than to product quality, **it is the least durable component of Microsoft's
competitive position — and it is precisely the component now under formal investigation in the
US, the UK and the EU simultaneously.**

## 6. The AI question, both directions

The brief asked for both cases at full strength. Here they are, then my adjudication.

### 6.1 The bull case at full strength

**Thesis: Copilot deepens the moat because AI is only as good as the data it can reach, and
Microsoft already holds the data.**

The argument is not weak and I will state it properly. A generative model's usefulness in an
enterprise is bounded not by its reasoning but by its *context*: the email thread, the SharePoint
document, the Teams meeting transcript, the Dynamics record, the identity graph that determines
who is permitted to see what. **Microsoft owns all of that plumbing and no competitor can
replicate it without the customer performing a migration they have already declined to perform
(§4).** An AI that can see your whole company is worth more than a better AI that can see
nothing. Permissioning alone — the Entra-mediated question of which documents this employee may
be shown — is a genuine, hard, compliance-critical asset.

**The supporting numbers are real and they are fast:**

| Metric | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| M365 Copilot paid seats | **15m** (Q2 FY26) → **20m** (Q3 FY26) → **>30m** (Q4 FY26) | [LicenseQ Q4](https://licenseq.com/microsoft-fy26-q4-results-explained/); [LicenseQ Q3](https://licenseq.com/microsoft-fy26-q3-results-explained/); [TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/322143/20260729/azure-tops-100b-copilot-paid-seats-jump-30m-microsoft-blowout-quarter.htm) |
| Q4 FY26 seat addition | **~10m in a single quarter** — fastest since launch | [GCN](https://gcn.com/microsoft-365-copilot-added-million-paying/20188/) |
| Fortune 500 penetration | **~70% of the Fortune 500 pay for M365 Copilot** | [SQ Magazine](https://sqmagazine.co.uk/copilot-statistics/) |
| Company AI annual run rate | **>$37bn, +123% YoY** (Q3 FY26) | [Microsoft FY26 Q3](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/earnings/fy-2026-q3/press-release-webcast) |
| GitHub Copilot | **>20m users, ~140,000 organisations**; enterprise customers **+55% QoQ** | [LicenseQ](https://licenseq.com/microsoft-fy26-q3-results-explained/) |
| Effect on the core franchise | **ARPU growth of ~10pp** on flat-to-growing seats, attributed by Microsoft to **E5 and Copilot** | §4.1 |

**The strongest form of the bull case is §4.1, not the seat count.** Copilot is already
demonstrably lifting revenue per seat in the legacy franchise without costing a single net seat.
That is the moat *monetising* an adjacent technology rather than being disrupted by it — the
outcome Intel failed to achieve (see `reports/equity/2026-07-28-intc-moat.md`).

### 6.2 The bear case at full strength — GenAI as the first credible threat to Office in 30 years

**Thesis: the Office franchise is not a document format, it is a user interface. If the interface
becomes a chat box, the incumbent UI is worth nothing, and thirty years of accumulated muscle
memory — the true switching cost — evaporates.**

State this at maximum strength, because it deserves it.

Word, Excel and PowerPoint were never protected by file formats — those have been reverse
engineered for two decades and LibreOffice reads them. They were protected by **the fact that
several hundred million people know where the buttons are, and retraining them costs more than
the licences.** Every prior challenger (StarOffice, OpenOffice, Google Docs, LibreOffice) had to
ask the user to relearn an interface, and every one of them failed at the enterprise scale for
that reason.

**Generative AI is the first challenger that does not ask the user to relearn anything. It asks
them to unlearn.** If the output of work is produced by describing what you want in natural
language, then:

- **The ribbon is a liability, not an asset.** Thirty years of feature accretion becomes thirty
  years of surface area a chat interface does not need.
- **The switching cost inverts.** The user does not need to know where the buttons are in the
  *new* product either. The retraining cost that protected Microsoft protects nobody.
- **The moat migrates from the application layer to the model layer** — a layer where Microsoft
  does not have a durable lead, is a *customer* rather than an owner (§7), and where the
  strongest brand is not Microsoft's.

**And the evidence that this is already happening is stronger than the Microsoft bulls
acknowledge:**

| Datum | Figure | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Copilot usage share where employees can choose** | **8%** when Copilot, ChatGPT and Gemini are all available. **68%** when Copilot is the *only* tool available | [ValueAdd VC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/microsoft-copilot-enterprise-adoption-what-the-data-shows-about-real-usage-vs-hype); [Stackmatix](https://www.stackmatix.com/blog/microsoft-copilot-enterprise-adoption-2026) |
| M365 Copilot **activation rate** | **35.8%** of provisioned users actively use it; independent surveys put **weekly** usage of purchased seats at **20–30%** | same |
| ChatGPT AI-chatbot web market share | **79.08%**, vs Microsoft Copilot **3.23%** (Gemini 7.03%, Perplexity 7.67%, Claude 2.98%) | [Business of Apps](https://www.businessofapps.com/data/microsoft-copilot-statistics/) |
| Paid AI subscriber share | ChatGPT **55.2%** vs Copilot **11.5%** (Recon Analytics, Jan 2026) | [Venuelabs](https://venuelabs.com/bing-statistics/) |
| **OpenAI enterprise footprint** | **>9 million paying business users**; ~**$5bn+ enterprise ARR** mid-2026, from ~$2.4bn end-2025; **92% of the Fortune 500** with at least one paid deployment | [ValueAdd VC](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/openai-enterprise-revenue-2026-what-we-know-about-contracts-seats-and-arr); [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/1-million-businesses-putting-ai-to-work/) |

*Sourcing caveat: the usage-share and activation figures come from aggregator sites and vendor
surveys of variable quality. They are the weakest sourcing in this report. I have not been able
to trace the 8%/68% figure to its primary methodology and it is registered in §15, item 5. I use
it because two independent aggregators carry it and because it is directionally corroborated by
the 35.8% activation figure and the chatbot share table. **If it is wrong, the bear case in this
section weakens materially and the committee should say so.***

**The 8%-versus-68% datum, if it holds, is the most important number in this report after ROIC.**
It says that Copilot's enterprise position is a function of *procurement*, not *preference*. An
IT department buys it because it is on the existing paper; employees use something else when
permitted. **A franchise that wins on procurement and loses on preference is a distribution
moat, not a product moat — and distribution moats are exactly what antitrust regulators
dismantle** (§9). That connection is the core of my thesis and I return to it in §12.

And note **92% of the Fortune 500 has a paid OpenAI deployment against Microsoft's ~70% Copilot
penetration.** OpenAI is *more* widely present in the largest US enterprises than Microsoft's own
AI product is. That is not a rounding error; that is a competitor inside the account.

### 6.3 Which does the evidence support?

**Both, on different time horizons, and the resolution is more interesting than either case
alone.**

**Near term (1–3 years): the evidence clearly supports the bull case, on revenue.** ARPU is up
~10pp on growing seats. Copilot went 15m → 30m paid seats in two quarters. AI run rate $37bn
+123%. **Whatever employees prefer, the money is being collected, and it is being collected
inside the existing contract.** No bear can argue with §4.1.

**Long term (5–10 years): the evidence supports the bear case, on the *nature* of the moat.**
Microsoft is not losing. But **the thing it is winning with has changed.** In 2015 Microsoft won
because Excel was the best spreadsheet and everyone knew how to use it. In 2026 Microsoft wins
because Copilot is already on the enterprise agreement and Entra already knows who you are. The
first is a product moat. The second is a distribution moat.

**My adjudication, stated as a claim the committee can hold me to:**

> **Generative AI has not yet damaged Microsoft's revenue and probably will not for several
> years. What it has already done is convert the Office moat from a preference moat into a
> distribution moat. Preference moats are durable and regulators leave them alone. Distribution
> moats are durable *until a regulator or a procurement policy removes the distribution* — and
> three regulators are currently working on exactly that (§9).**

That is why my trajectory call is **narrowing** rather than eroding: the mechanism has become
more fragile even though the numbers have not yet moved. Catching that gap is this desk's KPI.

## 7. The OpenAI relationship — asset or liability?

**Verdict: it was a decisive asset in 2023. As of the 27 April 2026 restructuring it is a
diminished asset, a P&L liability, an accounting distortion, and a disclosed dependency. Net,
today: roughly neutral, and deteriorating. It is the weakest-scoring component in §12 apart from
consumer.**

### 7.1 What actually changed — the terms

On **27 April 2026**, OpenAI and Microsoft published simultaneous posts announcing a rewrite of
the partnership
([the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/openai-and-microsoft-rewrite-their-deal-no-more-exclusivity-no-more-agi-clause/);
[TechRepublic](https://www.techrepublic.com/article/news-microsoft-openai-reset-partnership/);
[MindStudio](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/openai-microsoft-deal-restructured-4-terms-enterprise-ai);
[DDR Innova](https://ddrinnova.com/blog/openai-microsoft-multicloud-deal-2026/)).

| Term | Before | After (27 Apr 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| **Cloud exclusivity** | OpenAI on Azure | **Ended.** OpenAI may sell through any cloud provider. Azure retains **first availability** on new models only |
| **AGI clause** | Declaration of AGI could terminate the arrangement | **Removed** |
| **Microsoft's IP licence** | Exclusive | **Non-exclusive**, running **through 2032** |
| **Microsoft's equity** | — | **~27% of OpenAI Group PBC** |
| **Revenue share** | Microsoft paid a revenue share to OpenAI | **Microsoft no longer pays.** OpenAI continues paying royalties to Microsoft **through 2030** |

**The single most telling fact: OpenAI models appeared on AWS Bedrock the *next day*.** Not the
next quarter — the next day ([DDR Innova](https://ddrinnova.com/blog/openai-microsoft-multicloud-deal-2026/)).
The infrastructure to serve Microsoft's principal competitor had been built and was waiting for
the ink to dry.

### 7.2 The honest ledger

**What Microsoft kept, and it is not trivial:**
- A **non-exclusive licence to OpenAI IP through 2032** — six more years of the frontier models
  inside Copilot without owning the lab.
- **Royalties from OpenAI through 2030** and **no longer paying revenue share** — the cash flow
  direction reversed in Microsoft's favour.
- **~27% of an asset whose $13bn cost is now marked at roughly $135bn**
  ([WEEX](https://www.weex.com/questions/article/how-much-of-openai-does-microsoft-own-the-2026-reality-check-20476)).
  As a *financial* investment this has been one of the best in corporate history.
- **First availability on new models** — a real, if narrowing, product timing advantage.
- **Removal of the AGI clause is genuinely stabilising.** The scenario where OpenAI declares AGI
  and the commercial structure collapses is off the table. For enterprise buyers of Azure OpenAI
  services, that is a de-risking, and it is fair to count it as a positive.

**What Microsoft lost, and this is the moat-relevant column:**
- **Exclusivity — the entire basis of the 2023 advantage.** The thing that made Azure the only
  place to get GPT-class models is gone. Every Azure customer who chose Azure *for* OpenAI access
  can now get it on AWS.
- **The differentiator became a commodity input.** Microsoft is now one of several distributors
  of a supplier's product, competing on price and integration.
- **A P&L drag.** Under equity-method accounting Microsoft absorbs **27% of OpenAI's losses**. In
  one quarter this was a **$3.1bn hit to net profit, implying an OpenAI quarterly loss of roughly
  $11.5bn** ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/29/microsoft_earnings_q1_26_openai_loss/);
  [Longbridge](https://longbridge.com/en/news/263827430)).
- **An earnings-quality distortion.** Over nine months Microsoft recorded **$5.9bn of net gains
  from OpenAI, "primarily from a dilution gain"**, against $2.7bn of net *losses* in the prior
  comparable period ([Deep Quarry](https://deepquarry.substack.com/p/microsoft-starts-talking-about-openai);
  [om.co](https://om.co/2026/05/01/what-microsofts-10-q-says-about-openai/)). In Q4 FY26 the
  OpenAI investment **added $480m ($0.07/share) to GAAP earnings**, versus **subtracting $1.575bn
  ($0.21/share)** a year earlier ([The Investing Engineer](https://investingengineer.com/microsoft-fiscal-q4-2026-earnings-report/)).
  **A dilution gain is a non-cash accounting entry arising because OpenAI raised money at a
  higher valuation. It is not earnings. It substantially explains the 9pp gap between +31% GAAP
  and +22% non-GAAP net income growth in FY2026.**
- **RPO inflation.** §4.2: RPO +84% headline, **+25% ex-OpenAI**.

### 7.3 Is Microsoft building substitutes? Yes — visibly and urgently

| Fact | Source |
|---|---|
| At **Build 2026 (June)** Microsoft unveiled **seven MAI models**, including its first reasoning model, **MAI-Thinking-1** (sparse MoE, ~35bn active parameters, 256k context) | [FAQ.com.tw on Build 2026](https://faq.com.tw/en/developer-tools/2026-06-01-microsoft-build-2026-mai-coding-models-en/); [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/janakirammsv/2026/06/07/microsoft-builds-its-own-ai-stack-to-cut-openai-dependence/) |
| Microsoft claims MAI **matches Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.6 on coding tasks** | same |
| Microsoft is **replacing OpenAI and Anthropic models with in-house MAI models across Copilot products including Excel and Outlook** | [The Tech Portal](https://thetechportal.com/2026/07/07/microsoft-begins-replacing-openai-anthropic-models-with-in-house-mai-ai-across-key-products-report/); [the-decoder](https://the-decoder.com/copilot-goes-cheap-as-microsoft-phases-out-openai-and-anthropic-models-to-cut-costs/) |
| **But**: this is "still a small slice of total Copilot traffic. OpenAI and Anthropic handle most of it today." | same |
| Mustafa Suleyman (Microsoft AI chief): **"We pay a lot of money to Anthropic, so our goal is to reduce and ultimately eliminate that cost."** | [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/microsoft-bets-house-ai-cut-145500740.html) |

**Read the Suleyman quote as a moat analyst, not as a technologist.** It is an executive of the
world's most valuable software company stating publicly that **a major input cost is set by
third-party suppliers he wishes to eliminate.** Companies with wide moats do not talk about their
suppliers that way. **That sentence is a confession of input dependency**, and the fact that
Microsoft has had to license models from **Anthropic — a company substantially funded by Amazon,
its principal cloud competitor** — to keep Copilot competitive is the clearest available evidence
that Microsoft's model layer was not, and is not yet, a moat.

**The steel-man on the other side, at full strength:** Microsoft may not *need* to own the
frontier. If models commoditise — and multi-vendor routing inside Copilot is itself evidence they
are commoditising — then the value accrues to **whoever owns the distribution and the data**, and
that is Microsoft. Commoditisation of the model layer is arguably the *best* outcome for
Microsoft and the worst for OpenAI. **This is a genuinely strong argument and it may well be
right.** But it converts Microsoft's AI position into, once again, a distribution story (§6.3),
and it means the ~$135bn OpenAI mark is a financial windfall rather than a strategic asset.

### 7.4 Verdict

**Asset in 2023. Dependency in 2026.** Microsoft's privileged access has become non-exclusive
access; its differentiator has become an input it wants to stop buying; its equity stake produces
volatile non-cash GAAP swings that flatter or depress reported earnings without cash
consequences; and its flagship backlog metric is now materially a function of one counterparty's
commitments.

**The relationship is not a disaster and the ~$135bn mark is real money. But it is no longer a
moat source, and this report scores it accordingly (§12: 2 of 5).**

Windows OS licences and Surface devices are named among the **lagging** areas alongside Xbox

protected part and understates it simultaneously. **The correct statement is: the moat is
extremely deep and extremely narrow in scope. It covers the enterprise stack. It does not extend
one inch beyond it, and there is twenty years of evidence for that.**

## 9. Regulatory erosion — the attack is on the bundle, and the bundle is the moat

This is the section the brief correctly identified as decisive. **Three regulators, in three
jurisdictions, are attacking the same mechanism: the tie between Microsoft's monopoly products
and its contested ones.**

### 9.1 EU — Teams/Office: already binding, already conceded

| Item | Detail | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Date | **12 September 2025** — Commission accepted legally binding commitments, settling a case opened in 2023 | [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/09/12/microsoft-avoids-big-fine-as-eu-accepts-deal-to-unbundle-teams.html); [Slaughter and May](https://www.slaughterandmay.com/insights/new-insights/competition-and-regulatory-newsletter-european-commission-accepts-commitments-in-respect-of-microsoft-teams/) |

**Mitigating counter-evidence, at full strength:** Microsoft avoided a fine that could have run
to billions and did so on commitments it authored. Teams' enterprise position has not visibly
collapsed. **Microsoft has been extraordinarily good for twenty-five years at conceding the form
of a remedy while retaining its substance,** and the base rate for "EU remedy destroys Microsoft
franchise" is close to zero. The 2009 browser-choice-screen remedy did not save Internet
Explorer's rivals. **I weight this as material but not fatal.**

| Scale | **Over 70 subpoenas** issued | [Windows News](https://windowsnews.ai/article/ftcs-ongoing-microsoft-antitrust-probe-focuses-on-azure-and-ai-bundling-to-lock-out-competitors.421205) |
| Scope | Whether Microsoft **illegally ties Windows and Office to Azure and Copilot**; explicitly spans **cloud bundling, Entra ID, and Copilot** | same |
| Named mechanism | **"Azure Hybrid Benefit"** — discounts for on-premises licences used in Azure that are not available elsewhere | same |
| Rivals' complaint | Restrictive licensing and forced bundling lock competitors out of the enterprise market | same |

**The FTC has named the exact three assets this report identifies as the moat: Windows/Office
tying, Entra ID, and Copilot bundling.** That is not a coincidence — a regulator and a moat
analyst looking at the same business will identify the same load-bearing walls, because a moat
and an antitrust problem are frequently the same fact viewed from two directions.

### 9.4 The synthesis, and why this is the trajectory call

Microsoft's cloud position rests materially on a licensing toll — a **4–5x** price differential
for running its own server software on rival clouds (§5.2). Its AI position rests materially on
distribution rather than preference (§6.2). **Both of those are bundling.** And bundling is now
under simultaneous, active, formal challenge in the EU (concluded and binding), the UK (live SMS
case) and the US (70+ subpoenas).

> **The bundle is the moat. Three regulators are attacking the bundle. That is the definition of
> a narrowing moat, and it is narrowing before the numbers show it — which is what this desk is
> paid to identify.**

**The steel-man against my own call, stated fairly:** Microsoft has survived every antitrust
action in its history with its economics intact. The 2001 US consent decree did not stop Windows.
The 2009 EU browser remedy did not save Opera. Remedies are slow, negotiated, and typically
address the last war. **The base rate strongly favours Microsoft.** I weight this heavily — it is
why §12 gives regulatory durability 5 of 10 rather than 1 of 10 — but a base rate is not a
guarantee, and three concurrent actions aimed at the same mechanism is not the historical
pattern.

## 10. Threat assessment — top 2 credible threats

### Threat 1 (technology shift + changing customer behaviour): the interface moves to the model layer and Microsoft becomes a distributor — **Medium probability, Very high impact**

Detailed in §6.2 and §7. The compressed form: **Copilot's active usage share is 8% where
employees can choose and 68% where they cannot**; **only 35.8% of provisioned Copilot seats are
actively used**; **OpenAI has paid deployments in 92% of the Fortune 500 against Microsoft's ~70%
Copilot penetration**; and **Microsoft's own AI chief says the goal is to eliminate payments to
model suppliers.**

*Why only Medium probability:* the money is being collected regardless (ARPU +10pp, §4.1),
Microsoft's Entra/data plumbing is genuinely hard to replicate, and model commoditisation — which
is visibly under way — is the outcome that most favours the incumbent distributor.

*Why Very high impact:* if it happens, it does not damage a segment; it re-rates the entire
company from a software business earning 46.8% operating margins to an infrastructure business
earning much less. **§3 says that re-rating has already begun in the returns.**

**Leading indicator to monitor:** M365 Copilot **activation and weekly-active rates**, not seat
count. Seats are a procurement decision and lag reality by a renewal cycle; activation is a
preference decision and moves first. **If Microsoft stops disclosing seat counts, treat that as
the signal.**

### Threat 2 (regulation): simultaneous unbundling actions remove the licensing toll — **High probability of action, Medium impact**

Detailed in §9. The probability of *some* adverse remedy is high — the EU one has already
happened and is binding to 2032/2035; the CMA SMS case is live; the FTC has issued 70+ subpoenas
naming Entra ID and Copilot bundling explicitly.

The impact is rated Medium rather than High only because of the base rate: Microsoft has absorbed
every previous remedy without material economic damage. **But the specific mechanism at risk —
the 4–5x licensing differential — is, on Google's and CISPE's own account, a significant driver
of Azure's competitive position (§5.2). If it is removed, the honest question is how much of
Azure's share gain over AWS survives on product merit alone. Nobody, including me, knows the
answer, and that uncertainty is a moat discount in itself.**

### Threats considered and ranked below the top two

- **European digital-sovereignty migration** (§4.3) — 0.18% of seats today; price-insensitive and
  compounding. **Real, and my named erosion call, but a decade-scale threat, not a three-year
  one.**
- **Google Cloud growing at ~2x Azure's rate** (§5.2) — competitive, not existential; Google
  starts from ~13% share and the market is growing 35%.
- **Capex/memory cost cycle** — an earnings risk and a valuation risk (and the direct cause of the
  ROIC compression in §3), but a *cyclical* input-cost problem, not a structural moat threat.
  It belongs to 巴爺爺 and 𢦀鳩仔, not to me.
- **Depreciation-assumption risk** (§3.6) — an earnings-quality issue referred to Dept 2
  financial statements, not a moat issue. But if the 25-year useful life proves wrong, reported
  ROIC falls further from an already-halved level.

## 11. Ten-year test

**What must stay true for Microsoft to be a stronger business in 2036 than it is today:**

| # | Must stay true | Current status | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Enterprise identity (Entra) remains the un-migratable layer** — no large organisation demonstrates a clean exit | No documented large-scale displacement found | **High** |
| 2 | **ARPU expansion continues to outrun seat growth without triggering churn** — the §4.1 mechanism keeps working | FY26: revenue +16–19% on seats +6% | **High for 3 yrs, Medium for 10** |
| 3 | **AI capex converts into durable, high-margin revenue rather than a depreciating commodity asset base** — i.e. ROIC stabilises and the 5-year decline ends | ROIC down every year since FY21; gross margin lowest since 2022 | **Low-Medium — this is the swing factor** |
| 4 | **Copilot wins on preference, not only on procurement** — activation rates rise toward normal software levels | 35.8% activation; 8% usage share in head-to-head | **Low-Medium** |
| 5 | **The licensing toll survives, or Azure proves it does not need it** | Under active challenge in three jurisdictions | **Medium** |
| 6 | **Microsoft is not structurally dependent on a supplier for its flagship AI product** — MAI reaches parity and carries the majority of Copilot traffic | MAI shipped Jun 2026; still "a small slice" of traffic | **Medium** |
| 7 | **European sovereignty migration stays confined to the public sector and to document editing** | 800k workstations = 0.18% of seats; published sub-1-yr payback | **Medium** |
| 8 | **Capital allocation discipline holds** — no second Activision | Gaming being cut and divested; that is the right behaviour | **Medium-High** |

**Items 1, 2 and 3 are load-bearing.** If 1 and 2 hold — and I think they probably do — Microsoft
remains a formidable business in 2036 regardless of everything else in this report, because the
enterprise stack is genuinely the deepest switching-cost asset in this coverage universe.

**Item 3 is the whole argument.** If AI capex converts to durable high-margin revenue, ROIC
stabilises in the low-to-mid 20s and the moat is merely *wide and repriced*. If it does not —
if 2026–2028 turns out to be an arms race in which the participants collectively destroy the
economics they were defending — Microsoft ends the decade with a fortress balance sheet, an
intact customer base, and the return profile of a utility.

**My probability that Microsoft is a structurally stronger business in 2036 than today: 65–70%.
That is an estimate and it is mine, not sourced.** It is the highest ten-year confidence this
department has assigned in 2026 (BeOne: 55–60%). **But note carefully: "stronger business" is not
"wider moat."** My central expectation is that Microsoft in 2036 is **larger, more entrenched,
and less profitable on capital** — a bigger fortress with thinner walls.

### 12.2 Verdict: **WIDE moat · NARROWING**

**Why WIDE.** Three specific reasons, each numerical: (i) the ARPU-versus-seats evidence in §4.1
is the cleanest demonstration of pricing power this desk has documented in 2026; (ii) two decades
of ROIC far above WACC, with no expiry date — unlike BeOne's, this moat does not run out on a
known day; (iii) 450m+ paid seats with no documented displacement of the identity layer.

**Why NARROWING, not stable.** Five independent measures that do not share a single common cause:

1. ROIC down every year for five years, 45.6% → ~20.5–26.1%, **while the accounting was changed
   in the direction that flatters it**.
2. Gross margin at its lowest since 2022, with management attributing it to mix.
3. Azure demoted to middle grower of the big three in the June-26 quarter; AWS at an 18-quarter
   high.
4. The OpenAI exclusivity — the whole 2023 advantage — formally ended on 27 April 2026, with
   models on a competitor's platform within 24 hours.
5. The bundle is under simultaneous formal attack in three jurisdictions, with one remedy already
   binding.

**Why not ERODING.** Because the customers are *more* locked in than a year ago, not less. Seats
grew, ARPU grew ~10pp, ex-OpenAI RPO grew 25%. **Erosion means losing customers. Microsoft is
not losing customers; it is earning less on the capital required to keep them.** Those are
different diseases and the distinction is the point of this report.

**What would move me to WIDE/STABLE (74 → 80+):** two consecutive years of stable-or-rising ROIC
with capex growth decelerating; Copilot activation rates rising above ~60%; MAI models carrying
the majority of Copilot traffic; and the FTC matter closing without a conduct remedy.

**What would move me to NARROW (74 → sub-60):** M365 seat growth turning negative in any
developed region; a documented Entra ID displacement at a Fortune 500 company; an FTC or CMA
conduct remedy that removes the Windows Server/SQL Server licensing differential; or Azure
growth falling below AWS's for two consecutive quarters.

### 12.3 Calibration — why 74 and not 90, and what makes it stronger than 6160's 68

The brief requires me to justify any score above 68 rather than assert it. **Both halves of that
comparison need explaining, because the six-point gap will otherwise be misread.**

**Why Microsoft is genuinely stronger than BeOne (74 > 68), stated concretely:**

| | 6160.HK (BeOne) | MSFT |
|---|---|---|
| **Moat expiry** | **Dated. US exclusivity 2034–2037.** The moat ends on a known day | **Undated.** No patent cliff exists. The switching cost persists as long as the customer persists |
| **ROIC duration** | **Six quarters of GAAP profitability** — not "sustained" by my own rule | **Two decades** above cost of capital, including through 2008 and 2020 |
| **Share persistence** | Class revenue leadership achieved in 2025 — new, unproven across a cycle | #1 or #2 in every core enterprise category for 25+ years |
| **Customer captivity** | A prescription pad. Zero switching cost by construction | 450m+ paid seats; ~10pp ARPU increase absorbed without seat loss |
| **Binary risk** | A single Phase 3 readout (degraders) can halve the franchise | No single event can do comparable damage |

**Why the gap is only six points, which is the more interesting half:**

1. **The score measures the moat across the *whole* company.** Microsoft carries 25% of itself —
   search, consumer AI, gaming, devices — with **no moat and, in gaming, twenty billion dollars
   of value destruction.** BeOne carries no such dead weight. A conglomerate discount applies to
   moat scores as much as to valuations.
2. **Trajectory is the opposite in each case.** BeOne is widening on five independent measures.
   Microsoft is narrowing on five. **A 68 heading up and a 74 heading down will cross.**
3. **BeOne's moat is threatened by competitors. Microsoft's is threatened by regulators** — and
   regulators can act on a moat's *mechanism* directly, which no competitor can.

**Why not 90.** A 90 in this house would require what KLAC has: a rising ROIC, an expanding share
lead, and no live regulatory attack on the mechanism of advantage. **Microsoft has a falling
ROIC, a narrowing growth lead in its growth engine, and three concurrent antitrust actions aimed
precisely at the bundle.** A score in the high 80s would be describing Microsoft in 2021, not
Microsoft in 2026. **That is the specific consensus error this report exists to correct.**

**Explicit warning to the committee against a misreading:** 74 vs 68 does not mean Microsoft and
BeOne are near-equivalent assets. **The width verdicts differ — Wide vs Narrow — and that
categorical difference matters far more than six points on a scale.** The scale compresses at the
top by construction.

## 13. Margin of safety required

**I require a 20% margin of safety to 𢦀鳩仔's central intrinsic-value estimate — the lowest this
desk has demanded in 2026 — conditional on the modelling requirements below being met.**

**Derivation:**

| Factor | Effect on required MoS |
|---|---|

| Two decades of ROIC above WACC through multiple cycles | **Reduces materially** |

| Fortress balance sheet; disciplined divestment of losing units | **Reduces** |

| Capex denominator doubling; terminal invested capital genuinely unknown | **Increases materially** |
| Three concurrent regulatory actions on the moat's mechanism | **Increases** |
| GAAP earnings distorted by non-cash OpenAI marks; useful-life extensions in progress | **Increases** |
| Copilot activation evidence suggests the AI revenue may be less sticky than the seat count implies | **Increases** |

**Net: 20%.** Lower than BeOne's 30% because the moat has no expiry date and twenty years of
proof. Lower than KLAC's 25–30% because there is no cyclical revenue collapse scenario. **Not
lower than 20%, because the return on capital is falling and nobody — including management — has
demonstrated where it stops.**

**Binding guidance to the valuation team (𢦀鳩仔). I regard these as conditions on the 20%, not
suggestions:**

1. **Do not use GAAP net income.** FY2026 GAAP net income grew 31% and non-GAAP grew 22%; the gap
   is substantially non-cash OpenAI dilution gains (§7.2). **Use the non-GAAP series or strip
   "other income (expense), net" explicitly.**
2. **Do not capitalise the current free cash flow into perpetuity without a capex assumption you
   are prepared to defend.** Capex went from $64.55bn to ~$116bn in one year, with ~$175bn planned
   for calendar 2026. **The single largest source of valuation dispersion on this name is the
   terminal capex-to-revenue ratio, not the growth rate.** Run it as a scenario pair: capex
   normalising to a mid-teens percentage of revenue (bull) versus remaining above 30% (bear).
3. **Stress the depreciation assumption.** Server useful life 4→6 years; datacentre/office
   15→25 years from FY27 (§3.6). **Model a scenario in which server life reverts to 5 years and
   show the operating-income effect.** Refer to 巴爺爺 for adjudication.
4. **Value the moat and the AI infrastructure separately.** The enterprise software franchise
   earns software returns; the AI infrastructure earns infrastructure returns. **Applying a single
   blended multiple to $332bn of revenue is the central analytical error available on this name.**
5. **Haircut the AI run rate.** The $37bn AI ARR includes "passed-through OpenAI models" (§5.2).
   Until that is sized, do not treat the full figure as third-party enterprise demand.
6. **Do not use the $678bn RPO headline. Use the ex-OpenAI figure (+25%).**
7. **Reference price for the discount calculation: $484.31 (19 Aug 2026), market cap ~$3.6tn,
   forward P/E 24.4, 52-week range $349.20–$553.72.** A 20% MoS means I do not support initiation
   unless intrinsic value is at least ~$605 per share on 𢦀鳩仔's central case — i.e. price ≤ 80%
   of value. *(That threshold is arithmetic from my MoS requirement, not a valuation of my own —
   valuation is 𢦀鳩仔's mandate, not mine.)*

**Position implication (my view only; committee decides):** the moat supports **owning this at a
sensible price** and does not support **paying a 2021 multiple for a 2026 return profile.** It is
the highest-quality business this department has covered in 2026. It is also, uniquely among the
names covered, one where the *quality* is measurably declining while the *narrative* is not.
