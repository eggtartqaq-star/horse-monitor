## Structural analysis — this part does not depend on the missing data

### What a covered call does to a return distribution

Selling a call converts uncertain upside into a certain, smaller payment now. The
seller keeps the premium, **gives up gains above the strike, and keeps essentially
the entire downside**. Every month, the fund does this again.

That trade is not inherently bad. It is a deliberate exchange of *right-tail return*
for *current income*, and for the right investor it is entirely sensible.

### Why the horizon is the crux

Long-run equity returns are driven disproportionately by a small number of very large
upward moves. A strategy that systematically sells the right tail is, by construction,
selling the part of the distribution that produces most of the long-term compounding.

**An 18-year-old's single greatest financial asset is time — and time's value is
almost entirely in that right tail.** A covered-call overlay sells it, every month,
for forty years.

This is a structural argument, not a market forecast, and it stands regardless of
what HSCEI does next.

### Who this product genuinely suits

Stated fairly, because the fund is not defective — it is doing exactly what it says:

- someone who **needs income now** rather than growth later
- someone with **low volatility tolerance**
- someone with a **shorter horizon**
- someone who expects the underlying market to move **sideways**

An 18-year-old university student with a 40-year horizon and no income need matches
**none** of these.

### The 18% is not a return

A distribution yield is what the fund pays out. A total return is what the investor
actually earns: NAV change **plus** distributions. A fund can pay 18% while its NAV
falls, in which case part of that "yield" is the investor's own capital handed back.

The single reported total-return figure — **10.50% average annual since inception**
— is materially lower than the 18% distribution yield, which is consistent with the
distribution exceeding what the strategy earns. **One unverified source is not enough
to conclude that**, but it is the right number to chase.

### The compounding trap for a student specifically

Distributions arrive **monthly, in cash**. For compounding to work they must be
reinvested. For a student that means either:

- twelve extra small purchases a year, each paying a brokerage minimum on a tiny
  amount — the same fixed-cost-on-small-notional problem that decided the V6.4
  audit — or
- the cash sits idle, or gets spent.

**A monthly-paying product is one of the worst possible structures for someone whose
entire objective is to not touch the money for decades.**

## The cost question — arithmetic the Owner must complete

One fact that helps: **exchange-traded funds are exempt from Hong Kong's 0.1% stamp
duty.** That removes the largest statutory charge.

What remains is the **brokerage minimum**, and it is decisive:

| One-way brokerage min | As % of a HK$1,000 purchase |
|---|---|
| HK$0 (some app brokers) | 0.00% |
| HK$15 | 1.50% |
| HK$30 | 3.00% |
| HK$50 | 5.00% |
| HK$100 (typical bank) | **10.00%** |

*Illustrative brackets, not a quoted schedule — substitute the student's real broker.*

**At a traditional bank's minimum, a tenth of every deposit is gone before the market
opens.** At a zero-commission app broker the plan is fine on cost. This single fact
swings the answer more than any view on Chinese equities, and **only the Owner can
supply it.**

If the minimum is material, buying **quarterly instead of monthly** cuts the drag by
roughly two-thirds for the same annual contribution.
