# Bloom Energy (NYSE: BE) — Economic Moat Assessment
**Analyst:** 菲比斯, Economic Moat Analyst, Dept. 2 (Equity Research)
**Reference date:** 2026-07-19 · **Written:** 2026-07-25
**Last price used:** $226.26 (2026-07-21), market cap ~$61.1B, EV ~$61.6B, shares 284.4M

## 1. Moat rating

> ### **NARROW — and currently WIDENING, but the widening is CYCLICAL, not structural.**
> Sub-rating: **cyclically widening (2026–2029) → base case erosion begins 2029–2031.**
> I would underwrite the *narrow* rating. I would **not** underwrite the widening.

Bloom has, for the first time in 25 years, produced the *pattern* of a moat: rising gross
margin, a profitable aftermarket, a backlog 10x annual revenue, and a manufacturing scale
advantage over every other solid-oxide competitor. What it has **not** produced is the
*substance* of a moat: it has never earned its cost of capital, and roughly half of the
revenue driving the re-rating is booked to joint ventures Bloom itself co-owns.

The advantage that is actually working today — a 90-day behind-the-meter deployment against
4–7 year interconnection queues and gas turbine slots sold out to 2029/2030 — is a **scarcity
window**, not a barrier to entry. Windows close. Moats do not.

## 2. The ROIC test (mandate: no moat without ROIC evidence)

**Question posed by the CIO: has BE ever earned its cost of capital? Answer: No. Not in any
year, and not cumulatively, in 25 years of operation.**

| Metric | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Accumulated deficit, 31-Dec-2025 | **$(3,986.98)M** | FY2025 10-K |
| Accumulated deficit, 31-Dec-2024 | $(3,897.62)M | FY2025 10-K |
| ⇒ Implied FY2025 net loss | **~$(89.4)M** — *in the "record" year* | derived |
| Total stockholders' equity, 31-Dec-2025 | $792.96M | FY2025 10-K |
| ⇒ Implied lifetime contributed equity capital | **~$4.8B** (est. = deficit + equity) | derived, estimate |
| FY2025 revenue | $2,020M (+37.3% y/y) | Q4'25 release |
| FY2025 GAAP operating income | **$72.8M** (3.6% margin) — best year ever | Q4'25 release |
| FY2025 gross margin | 29.0% (vs 27.5% FY24) | Q4'25 release |
| TTM GAAP net income (implied by 10,135x trailing P/E on $61.1B cap) | **~$6M** | stockanalysis.com |

**Pre-tax return on cumulative contributed capital, FY2025 (best year in company history):**
$72.8M operating income ÷ (~$4.8B contributed equity + ~$1.2B debt) ≈ **~1.2%.**
Against an estimated WACC of 10–12% for a high-beta, pre-cash-tax hardware company, Bloom's
peak year cleared roughly **one-tenth** of its cost of capital. Cumulatively the figure is
deeply negative: $4.0B of shareholder capital has been converted into a business that
generated $6M of trailing GAAP net income.

**The honest counter-argument — forward ROIC is a different animal.** The business model has
flipped from asset-heavy to asset-light. Historically Bloom owned and financed installations
(the Electricity segment); now it *sells boxes* to Brookfield-sponsored JVs that carry the
capital. As of Q1 2026: cash $2.52B, total debt ~$2.60B — i.e. **net invested capital of only
~$1.0–1.6B (estimate)** against a Q1'26 GAAP operating income run-rate of ~$289M annualised
($72.2M x 4) and a non-GAAP run-rate of ~$519M. On that base, **forward ROIC screens at
25–40%.**

That number is real arithmetic but it is *not yet evidence of a moat*, for three reasons:
1. It is one quarter old and rests on 2026 guidance ($3.4–3.8B revenue, ~34% non-GAAP GM).
2. It is flattered by pushing the capital intensity into JVs that Bloom co-owns — the capital
   did not disappear, it moved off Bloom's balance sheet while Bloom keeps an equity stake in
   its losses (see §4.2).
3. Near-zero cash tax (NOL shield from the $4B deficit) inflates cash NOPAT versus a
   normalised owner's return.

**Verdict on the ROIC test: FAILED on history, UNPROVEN on the forward. Under my own rule —
"no moat without ROIC evidence" — Bloom cannot be rated above narrow, and the narrow rating
is granted on structural/technical grounds (§3), not on returns.**

## 3. Evidence by moat source

### 3.1 Intellectual property / process technology — **NARROW (real)**
- **Strongest supporting evidence:** *Competitors are quitting.* FuelCell Energy **abandoned
  its SOFC platform in June 2025**, taking a $64.5M impairment. Doosan Fuel Cell's new SOFC
  plant (Gunsan, commissioned July 2025) runs at **50 MW/year** — 2.5% of Bloom's planned 2 GW.
  Ceres Power only launched its Endura SOFC platform on 2026-04-18 and monetises via licence
  (Weichai for China, Centrica for Europe) rather than manufacturing. The $4.0B accumulated
  deficit is itself the entry barrier: it is the price of the learning curve, and a new
  entrant must pay it.
- **Strongest counter-evidence:** Energy-hardware technology leads have a poor record of
  durability — solar, li-ion and wind all saw Western first-movers with 10-year technical
  leads commoditised inside a decade. The Ceres/Weichai China licence is precisely the vector
  by which that happens. Bloom's edge sits in manufacturing yield and field data, which is
  copyable with capital and time, not in an unassailable patent thicket.

### 3.2 Cost advantage / scale — **NARROW at best, UNPROVEN**
- **Support:** Gross margin has climbed 27.5% (FY24) → 29.0% (FY25) → 30.0% (Q1'26), with
  ~34% non-GAAP guided for FY26. Management claims ~10%/year unit cost-down with scale
  economics "not yet kicked in." Fremont expansion 1 GW → 2 GW for ~$100M capex — an
  extraordinarily cheap doubling, and >10x any rival SOFC line.
- **Counter:** A 30% gross margin in a market where the customer's alternative is *waiting
  four years* is **scarcity pricing, not cost advantage**. There is no published evidence that
  Bloom's installed $/kW beats a gas turbine's; the honest read is that it does not, and that
  Bloom wins on schedule and permits. Absolute cost leadership is unproven and, on capex/kW
  versus combined-cycle gas at scale, likely false.

### 3.3 Switching costs / installed base annuity — **NARROW**
- **Support:** Stacks are proprietary; only Bloom can replace them. Service has been
  **profitable eight consecutive quarters**, 20% gross margin in Q4'25, and of the ~$20B total
  backlog, **~$14B is service** — a genuine contracted annuity under long-term service
  agreements (LTSAs).
- **Counter:** (a) 20% service gross margin is *thin* for a captive-parts monopoly — turbine
  OEM aftermarket runs 30–40%+. That gap says stack replacement is expensive, not lucrative.
  (b) Field-reported stack replacement cycles of 3–5 years sit against a claimed 20–25 year
  system life; if the field number is the true number, the $14B service backlog is a **cost
  liability disguised as revenue**. (c) Switching costs are *retrospective* — they defend
  installed sites but win nothing at the next data centre.

### 3.4 Regulatory / permitting barrier — **NARROW, valuable now, not proprietary**
- **Support:** Near-zero NOx/SOx/particulates lets Bloom permit in non-attainment air basins
  (Santa Clara, Northern Virginia, Phoenix, Chicago) where reciprocating gensets and, often,
  simple-cycle turbines cannot get a timely permit. Oracle's Project Jupiter reportedly pivoted
  from turbines-plus-diesel to a **100% Bloom fully-islanded microgrid** — no interconnect, no
  diesel, no turbines, no batteries. That is the single most impressive competitive datapoint
  in the file.
- **Counter:** This is **regulatory arbitrage available to any low-emission technology**, not a
  company-specific barrier. Every SOFC entrant inherits it. And it cuts both ways: air rules
  can be relaxed for "national AI infrastructure" as easily as tightened.

### 3.5 Network effects — **NONE.** No credible mechanism.

### 3.6 Brand — **NONE-to-NARROW.** Reference-customer value in hyperscaler procurement, but
these buyers select on delivery date and $/MWh, not brand.

### 3.7 First-mover in behind-the-meter power — **NARROW and explicitly time-limited**
~90-day deployment against interconnection queues of 4–7 years. GE Vernova's gas turbine
backlog reached **116 GW in Q2 2026** (up from 100 GW in Q1); OEM lead times run ~3 years with
slots opening in 2029–2030, and industry-wide quotes stretch as far as 8 years. **This is the
entire thesis, and it is a dated option, not a moat.**

## 4. Threat assessment

### 4.1 Threat #1 — The scarcity window closes (2029–2031). Probability: HIGH.
Bloom's pricing power is a function of turbine unavailability. All three major turbine OEMs are
adding capacity into a 100 GW+ backlog; GEV already has 2029/2030 slots to sell. Add grid
interconnection reform, restarted nuclear, and SMRs arriving in the early 2030s. When a
hyperscaler can buy a CCGT block on a 24-month schedule, procurement reverts to $/MWh and
capex/kW — where fuel cells have never won. **Steel-manned disruption case:** Bloom is not a
new power paradigm; it is the world's most sophisticated *bridge generator*, and bridges are
demolished when the road opens. In that world Bloom terminal-values as a specialty genset OEM
at 15–20% gross margin and 6–8x EBITDA, with an installed-base service annuity as the only
durable asset. Note this cuts against the bull case's own logic: if the "power scarcity" is
real and permanent, capital floods in and it stops being scarce; if it is temporary, Bloom's
window is 4–5 years.

### 4.2 Threat #2 — Revenue quality: related-party circularity. Probability: LIVE NOW.
This is the finding that most changes my rating, and the committee must see it.
- FY2025 related-party revenue: **$892.0M = 44.1% of total revenue**, of which **$862.1M came
  from Brookfield "Fund JVs" that did not exist before August 2025.**
- Q4 2025: **$574.2M of $777.7M revenue = 73.8% related-party.**
- Q1 2026: **~$373M of $751.1M = ~50% related-party.**
- Bloom recognised a **$19.6M equity loss from the unconsolidated Brookfield AI-infrastructure
  affiliate on an initial investment of only $24.6M.**

Read that last line carefully. Bloom books ~30% gross margin selling equipment to a vehicle it
part-owns, and then takes back the vehicle's losses through the equity line — losses running at
~80% of the invested stake in a partial period. That is the signature of **a financing
structure being reported as demand.** The **$25B Brookfield commitment (2026-06-30, a 5x
expansion of the original $5B framework)** must therefore be read as a *capital facility*, not
as an arm's-length order book. A moat is validated by independent customers paying up. Half of
Bloom's revenue is currently not independently validated. (Short-seller work — Hunterbrook's
"Bloom's Big Lie" — raises the same structure; I could not fetch the primary piece and cite it
here only as an existence flag, not as evidence.)

### 4.3 Threat #3 (honourable mention) — AI capex digestion.
The ~$6B product backlog (2.5x y/y) is only as firm as hyperscaler capex plans. A single
quarter of AI capex guidance cuts re-prices both the backlog and the multiple.

## 5. Ten-year test — what must stay true for BE to be *stronger* in 2036

Six conditions. I score four as unproven and two as currently failing.

| # | Condition | Threshold | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Scarcity converts to annuity | ≥8–10 GW installed base by 2032, service GM **≥30%**, demonstrated stack life ≥7 yrs | ✗ service GM 20%; stack life contested |
| 2 | Margin survives turbine normalisation | Product GM ≥30% sustained *after* 2030 | Unproven — the whole ballgame |
| 3 | Revenue becomes arm's-length | Related-party <25% of revenue by FY2028 | ✗ 44% FY25, ~50% Q1'26 |
| 4 | Self-funding | Positive FCF after capex through a full cycle, **no further dilution** | ✗ 205.7M sh (2022) → 284.4M (Jul-26), +38% |
| 5 | Cumulative ROIC crosses WACC | ~**$600M/yr sustained NOPAT** to earn 10% on ~$6B lifetime capital | ~4x the FY26 GAAP operating run-rate |
| 6 | No scaled SOFC rival | No competitor >500 MW/yr at comparable cost | Watch Ceres/Weichai (China), Doosan, Mitsubishi |

**If 1, 2 and 3 all hold, Bloom is a wide-moat industrial in 2036** (proprietary installed base
+ captive aftermarket + genuine scale cost lead) and today's price is defensible.
**If 2 or 3 fails, the moat is NONE** and Bloom is a cyclical equipment maker that happened to
sell into one extraordinary five-year window.

I put roughly **25–30%** subjective probability on the wide-moat branch. That is not a
knock on the company — it is an honest statement about a firm that has never earned its cost of
capital and whose largest customer is a fund that also finances it.

## 6. Implication for the margin of safety — the number for 𢦀鳩仔

### **Required margin of safety: 60% discount to central intrinsic value.**
### Buy only at **≤40% of appraised value**. Hard position cap **1.5% of NAV**.

Firm calibration for context: ~25–30% MoS for a proven wide-moat compounder; ~40% for a
narrow-moat business with demonstrated ROIC > WACC. Bloom is **pre-moat/early-moat with
compromised revenue quality and zero history of excess returns**, so it takes the top of the
scale plus a size cap. The size cap is not optional: the related-party concentration in §4.2 is
a fraud-adjacent *structure* risk (I make no allegation), and structure risks are managed by
position size, not by price.

**Implied-expectations check (estimate; assumptions stated):** at EV $61.6B, a 20x terminal
EV/EBIT multiple implies ~**$3.1B of steady-state EBIT**. At a generous 15% through-cycle EBIT
margin that is ~**$20B of revenue** — roughly **6x the FY2026 guide**, or ~8–13 GW/yr of
sustained shipments at $1.5–2.5M/MW. That is **4–6x the 2 GW capacity Bloom is spending to
reach by end-2026**, in perpetuity, with no margin compression when turbines free up. At $226
and ~17x EV/2026E sales and 85x forward earnings, **the current price contains no margin of
safety at all** — it prices the wide-moat branch at close to 100% probability against my 25–30%.

**Concrete guidance to Valuation:**
1. Build three scenarios and weight them: Wide (30%), Narrow-fading (45%), None (25%).
2. My MoS rule is satisfied when the **bear/None case still returns ≥0%** from the entry price
   — i.e. pay approximately the bear-case value and let the option be free.
3. Sanity anchor for the None case: normalised 2030 revenue at ~2 GW/yr, 20% gross margin,
   12% NOPAT margin, ~2.5x EV/Sales.
4. **Do not size up before the FY2026 10-K shows related-party revenue declining as a
   percentage of total.** That single line is the highest-information disclosure in the file.

**Erosion call, for the KPI record (made ahead of the numbers):** I expect Bloom's product
gross margin to peak in FY2027–FY2028 and the moat trajectory to flip from widening to eroding
in **2029–2031**, as gas turbine slots free up from 2029/2030 and at least one SOFC competitor
(most likely a Ceres-licensed Chinese manufacturer) reaches >500 MW/yr. Log this and score me
on it.
